The testbed is built so that the message channel is the only thing that differs between the compared teams. Agents, sensing, planning and random numbers are shared. Everything is scripted; there is no learned component and no language model.

\subsection{World and queries}
Each episode draws a 30$\times$30 grid that is split recursively into 4 to 8 rectangular rooms separated by walls with one or two doors. Rooms carry names from a fixed list (kitchen, lounge, office, \dots). A room is covered by a roof with probability 0.3; the aerial robot cannot see into covered rooms. Each room holds one or two landmarks (sofa, table, bed, shelf, desk), and the world holds 12 small objects: the target, two distractors and nine fillers. Objects belong to 20 classes organised in 5 groups of visually similar classes (for example mug, bottle, glass, bowl), have one of 6 colours, and are described as \emph{near} a landmark when one lies within Chebyshev distance two. The floor plan (walls, room names, roofs) is known to both robots; object positions are not.

A query is drawn from one of four tiers of increasing distance from a closed class vocabulary:
\emph{exact} (``the mug''), \emph{synonym} (``the cup'', a word outside the class list),
\emph{attribute} (``the red mug'', with two mugs of other colours present), and
\emph{relational} (``the mug near the sofa'', with two mugs away from any sofa present).
In the exact and synonym tiers the two distractors are other classes of the target's similarity group, so that the amount of clutter is the same in all tiers.

\subsection{Simulated open-vocabulary detector}
Real open-vocabulary detectors such as OWL-ViT~\cite{minderer2022simple} score image regions against free text. We replace the image side by a symbolic percept and keep the text-similarity side. Every class, synonym, colour and landmark word has a fixed random 64-dimensional unit embedding. Classes of one group share a common component, and a synonym is a perturbed copy of its class vector, so that a synonym query matches its class less sharply than the exact name does. A percept is a triple (label, colour, landmark). Its score against a query is the mean, over the slots the query mentions, of the cosine between the query word and the perceived word; a slot the percept does not know scores one half, and an absent landmark scores zero. Gaussian noise is added to the score (standard deviation 0.05 for the ground robot and 0.10 for the aerial robot).

\subsection{Robots}
The \emph{ground robot} moves one cell per step on the 4-connected free grid, senses the cells of its own room within Chebyshev radius 3, and perceives label, colour and position exactly. Objects whose noisy score reaches 0.75 become its own candidates; it walks to the nearest one and verifies it at close range, which takes 3 steps and is exact.

The \emph{aerial robot} moves 2 cells per step over walls and senses a square footprint of radius 4 except under roofs. Its perception is coarse: the label is replaced by another class of the same group with probability 0.2, the colour is perceived with probability 0.7, and positions are quantised to blocks of 3$\times$3 cells, which also makes its landmark relations approximate. It cannot verify from altitude. Once its own coverage is complete it falls back to descending on its best candidates, which costs 10 steps each.

Both robots explore with nearest-frontier coverage in the spirit of frontier exploration~\cite{xu2022explore}: the goal is the nearest cell not yet sensed, kept until it is sensed, with a cost penalty on cells of the room the teammate last announced. When no unsensed cell remains the coverage map is reset and a new pass starts with fresh detector noise. An episode succeeds when either robot verifies the target within 100 steps.

\subsection{Message channels}
A robot may send one message every $K=\,$5 steps. A message carries at most $M=\,$2 sightings and, when it changed, the sender's intent (where it is heading). The aerial robot reports every object whose \emph{class} score reaches 0.6, best first; it does not filter on colour or relation, because its perception of those is unreliable, and leaves that judgement to the receiver. The ground robot acts on its own sightings and sends only intents. The receiver turns each reported sighting into a candidate \emph{region}, re-scores what the message says against the query with the detector's scorer, drops entries below 0.7, and ranks the rest by score minus travel distance divided by 50. A region is resolved once the ground robot has sensed all of it. The channels differ only in how a sighting record is written and read:

\textbf{NL messaging (ours).} The speaker fills an English template, for example ``i am heading to the office. i see a red mug near the sofa in the kitchen and a mug in the lab''. Colour and landmark are mentioned when perceived. The location is the room name. The listener is a rule-based keyword parser (a mock language model): it scans the words, opens an entry at each class word, attaches colour, landmark and room words, and discards entries without a class or a location. The candidate region is the whole named room.

\textbf{Symbolic messaging (baseline).} The message is a list of fixed-schema tuples: \texttt{(G, x, y)} for intent and \texttt{(S, class\_id, x, y)} for a sighting. The schema has no slot for colour or relation, so the receiver scores those slots as unknown. The candidate region is the block around the coordinates.

\textbf{Decomposition variants.} To separate what a message says from how it says it we add three channels: \emph{NL + coordinates} (the same sentence with ``at x y'' inserted), \emph{Symbolic + attributes} (two more fields: colour id and landmark id), and \emph{Symbolic + confidence} (one more field: the sender's own full score, which the receiver uses instead of re-scoring).

\textbf{Information match and mismatch.} All channels encode the same sighting record. NL and the basic schema are deliberately \emph{not} matched in content: NL carries colour and relation but only a room-level location, while the schema carries a block-level location but no colour or relation. This is the trade-off the hypotheses are about. NL + coordinates and Symbolic + attributes carry identical information; because the template and parser are lossless without corruption, these two are behaviourally identical in clean conditions, and we report both only to make that point explicit. Budgets are matched in messages and sightings per message, not in tokens: an NL message uses about twice as many tokens (words) as a symbolic one uses fields (Table~\ref{tab:main}).

\textbf{Corruption.} For H3 each NL room word is replaced by another room name with probability $p$ and then each word is dropped with probability $p$. The symbolic analogue replaces each coordinate pair by a random cell with probability $p$ and then erases each field with probability $p$; a tuple with an erased required field is discarded.
