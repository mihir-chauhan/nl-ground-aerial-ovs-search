A ground robot and an aerial robot make a natural search team. The aerial robot covers area quickly but sees coarsely and cannot look under cover; the ground robot is slow but can identify an object at close range. When the target is specified in free text (``the red mug'', ``the mug near the sofa''), the two must also exchange what they see. Recent systems let robots do this in natural language, often through large language models~\cite{cladera2025air,yu2024mhrc,chen2023scalable,zhang2023building}. Language is attractive because it is open-ended: a sentence can mention a colour, a relation or a room without anyone having designed a field for it.

Whether that open-endedness pays off in task performance is a separate question from whether a full language stack works. A sentence and a tuple can carry the same facts, and a sentence typically refers to places loosely (``in the kitchen'') where a tuple gives coordinates. The research question of this study is therefore narrow: \emph{in a simulated grid world, does natural-language communication between a ground robot and an aerial robot improve open-vocabulary object search success and time-to-find compared with structured symbolic messaging and with non-communicating frontier exploration?}

The approved brief fixed three hypotheses before any experiment. \textbf{H1}: the natural-language team reaches higher success and fewer steps than the symbolic team at equal message budget. \textbf{H2}: the advantage grows as queries become more open-vocabulary and vanishes or reverses for exact class names. \textbf{H3}: the advantage shrinks under message corruption.

We test them in a deliberately small setting: a procedurally generated grid world, a simulated open-vocabulary detector, scripted frontier agents, and a templated language channel with a rule-based parser standing in for a language model. This keeps every factor other than the channel identical and lets us pair episodes across systems. It also bounds what can be claimed: the study is about the information format of messages, not about language models.

\textbf{Findings.} H1 is not supported for time-to-find: pooled over query tiers the language team is slightly slower than the symbolic team, with a small success gain. H2 is supported in direction: the language-minus-symbolic difference moves in favour of language from closed to open query tiers (interaction \rhval{paired/nl-minus-symbolic-open-minus-closed-tiers/all/d_steps/mean:2} [\rhval{paired/nl-minus-symbolic-open-minus-closed-tiers/all/d_steps_lo/mean:2}, \rhval{paired/nl-minus-symbolic-open-minus-closed-tiers/all/d_steps_hi/mean:2}] steps). H3 as stated presupposes an advantage that we did not observe; under corruption both channels lose and the gap does not move toward the symbolic channel. A decomposition shows that what helps is the attribute and relation \emph{content}, which a slightly richer schema carries equally well, while the room-level reference of our sentences is what costs time.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A reproducible testbed in which only the inter-robot message channel changes. (ii) A controlled comparison of templated language and fixed-schema messaging across four query tiers with paired layouts. (iii) A content-versus-format decomposition and ablations on message budget, corruption, aerial resolution, detector noise and team composition, including a coordination failure of our own design that the ablation exposed.
